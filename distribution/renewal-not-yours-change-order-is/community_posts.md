# Community Cross-Posts -- The Renewal Is Not Yours to Own. The Change Order Is.

## Slack (Gain Grow Retain / CS Insider)

Something's been bugging me about how we talk about renewals. We keep saying the CSM "owns" the renewal. But do we? It gets decided in a finance review we're not invited to, against pricing we didn't set, in a quarter shaped by a reorg no health score predicted. What we actually own is all the out-of-contract stuff we absorb for free. The 9pm decks. The unpaid dashboards. In professional services those are all priced change orders. We just call them "the relationship" and eat the cost. And now boards are looking at all that unpriced generosity and thinking a bot could do it cheaper. ## Reddit (r/CustomerSuccess)

**Title:** Is anyone else noticing that the favors we do for free are exactly what makes us look replaceable?

I've been in enterprise CS for a while and something clicked recently. We pride ourselves on "doing whatever it takes." The custom dashboard before a board meeting. The training session that wasn't in scope. The exec deck at 9pm because a sponsor asked nicely.

We call all of it "the relationship."

But in professional services, every single one of those is a priced change order. A documented line item someone signs off on. CS took the do-whatever-it-takes ethos from PS and imported it wholesale, but we left behind the discipline that made it sustainable. Nobody prices it. Nobody even counts it.

And here's the uncomfortable part. Now that vendors are pitching retention-as-a-service and boards are asking whether a machine can just run the renewal motion, we have no data to argue with. We never made our human labor legible. So the board can't tell which accounts genuinely need a person and which don't.

The thing I'm going to try: a favor ledger. Just logging every out-of-contract request for a quarter and totaling the hours per account. You can't defend work you never counted.

Curious if anyone here has actually tried tracking their unpriced work, and whether it changed how leadership saw the role. Kuber's Churn Is Dead newsletter got me thinking about this.
