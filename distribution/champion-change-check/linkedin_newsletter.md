# Your Champion Left. A New Contact Won't Replace Them.

Your champion leaves. Someone else accepts the meeting.

Would you leave the rollout date unchanged?

I'd want to know what the departing person made possible first. Knowing the product, wanting it to succeed and being able to commit a team's time are three different things. The new contact might have one of those. They might have all three. An introduction doesn't tell us.

That is where a contact handover can look complete while the customer plan is still missing something important.

## Start with the next commitment

I wouldn't begin by asking someone to become the new champion. I'd choose the nearest real customer commitment and work backwards from it.

Say the plan is to trial a reporting workflow with a second department. The person who left may have persuaded the manager to release staff time. They may also have understood the reporting problem and found the technical person who could fix the export.

Those jobs don't automatically move to the next person on the organisation chart. They don't all need to move to one person, either.

Write down what needs doing: approve test time, confirm the goal, resolve the technical question. A name beside a job title is less useful than knowing who can do each of those things.

## A willing contact can still have a boundary

“They're really engaged” might mean they know the product well and answer every email. That is useful. It doesn't establish that they can approve a rollout or defend its funding.

Microsoft's Dynamics 365 guidance distinguishes champions, end users, economic buyers and other stakeholder roles. It is sales-product guidance, not evidence that a contact model prevents churn. The useful distinction is between being involved and holding a particular role in a decision.

For the next customer commitment, I'd check:

- Who understands whether the proposed work is usable?
- Who is willing to help it move forward internally?
- Who can approve the time, money or change it requires?

Sometimes one person covers all three. Sometimes the work is shared. The answer can be simple; it just needs to be real.

If the new contact cannot make the decision, that isn't automatically resistance or low engagement. They may already be doing everything their role allows.

## Don't ask them to inherit the old business case

Give the new contact the short version: what was agreed, what actually happened and what is still open. Then let them correct it.

An old agreement belongs in the account history. It isn't a fresh commitment from people who weren't in that conversation.

GitLab's public Customer Success handbook describes success plans as mutually agreed, with outcomes, milestones, timelines and responsible people connected. After a departure, I'd check those connections again. The goal might survive while the team assigned to it has changed. The executive might still support it while nobody owns delivery.

That is a more useful conversation than another general product overview.

Try a small request: “The next step needs the operations team to test the export. Is that still useful, and who can agree the time for it?”

They can identify an owner, change the task or tell you the goal is no longer active. Any of those answers gives you something to work with.

## Three choices, applied to one request

Here's the check I'd use. It's an editorial decision aid, not a validated score or a renewal prediction.

**Continue:** the goal still matters, someone has accepted the next task and the necessary authority is available. Update the people and confirm the date.

**Change:** the goal survives but the old route doesn't. Agree a smaller or different step with the customer. Describe the new plan accurately.

**Hold the affected request:** nobody can yet approve its resources, confirm its priority or accept its commitment. Name what is waiting and who can resolve it. Keep unrelated support and working parts of the service moving.

Microsoft's Power Platform sponsorship guidance makes the underlying work concrete: sponsors help prioritise resources, approve funding and make decisions. It also recommends a backup sponsor. That doesn't mean every account needs a C-level relationship. It means this particular commitment needs someone with the authority to make it happen.

## What I'd change in the account update

Consider the second-department trial again. This is a fictional example, not a customer story.

The new contact is happy to meet, but the second team's manager hasn't agreed test time.

“New champion engaged; rollout on track” turns a meeting into evidence of a commitment that hasn't been made.

A more useful update would say:

“The operations lead still supports the goal. The next team's manager has not agreed test time. The contact will help arrange that conversation. The trial date is unconfirmed until the manager decides.”

Now the next action is clear. We don't need to label the contact as the problem or pretend that another friendly meeting resolves it.

## Take one plan through the check

Pick one live commitment. Record what the departing person enabled, what is still covered, what needs confirmation, who can decide and the next date. Choose what continues, changes or waits.

Keep it in the existing account record. Don't put private customer details into a public worksheet, and don't invent a renewal probability from a staffing change.
