# Title: Why Renaming Your Customer Success Team Won't Fix Your Retention Problem

One AI company ran a job posting for a "head of customer success." In two and a half months, they got two candidates. Two.

They changed one thing. The title. "Head of customer success" became "forward deployed engineer," a name borrowed from Palantir's technical deployment teams. The pipeline filled overnight.

That story, told by Ryan Seams of Assembly AI at a recent industry conference, has been passed around every post-sales leadership channel I know. And I understand why. It confirms a feeling a lot of leaders have been carrying for a year. The words "customer success" have gone quietly toxic with technical buyers and technical candidates alike.

The rebrand works for hiring. I won't pretend otherwise. But there's a second half to this story that doesn't fit on a conference slide, and it's the half that matters.

## The funnel that filled and the one that didn't

When you change the title, one funnel fills up. The candidate funnel. More people apply. Better people apply. Great.

The other funnel does not change at all. The customer relationship. The renewal. The moment where someone on the other side of the table decides whether to keep paying you.

And that second funnel is the one that actually pays your bills.

A title change is a refinancing move. You're taking a debt the role already ran up and moving it somewhere else, hoping the balance doesn't come due before you get credit for the reorg.

To understand what I mean by debt, you have to understand what actually broke.

## It was never just a bad word

Most of the conversation treats "customer success" like a term that simply aged badly. Like calling someone a "webmaster" today. The assumption is that the word got stale and a fresh one will fix it.

That's the wrong diagnosis.

When the customer success role first appeared, the title was accurate. It described a person whose job was to make sure customers got value. An advocate for the customer, sitting inside the vendor.

Then the job changed underneath the word.

Over the last several years, customer success teams started reporting to the Chief Revenue Officer. Net revenue retention, essentially how much more money existing customers spend over time, became the single number that mattered at the board level. Variable compensation got attached to expansion. Suddenly the person sold to the customer as their advocate was being measured, promoted, and paid on how much more they could sell that same customer.

The title stayed the same. The incentives flipped.

## Buyers are not stupid

Here's the part leaders underestimate. Buyers figured this out.

Not by reading your compensation plan. By living through the experience. They sat across from enough account managers whose friendly "strategic check-in" always drifted toward seat counts and usage tiers by minute forty. They noticed that the person introduced as their partner had a sales quota with their company's logo on it.

So when a sharp technical buyer hears "customer success" now, it doesn't signal incompetence or fluff. It signals something more specific. A commercial motion in a relationship costume. A sales conversation pretending to be a support conversation.

That is the credibility debt. The gap between what the role promises and what the customer experiences.

## Why the rebrand doesn't touch it

Rename the role to "forward deployed engineer" and you've chosen a word the customer hasn't learned to distrust yet. The word sounds technical. It sounds like someone building and shipping value, not chasing a number.

For a while, that works.

Then the renewal arrives. Your forward deployed engineer, it turns out, also has expansion targets. The same conversation happens. The same drift toward more seats and higher tiers. And now the word "engineer" has picked up the exact stain the word "success" carried.

You didn't pay down the debt. You moved it to a new account and started paying interest again.

## The audit to run before the memo

Before anyone drafts a reorg memo, run a one-page audit. For each customer-facing role, map three things.

**What they were promised.** What did your sales team say this role would do for the customer?

**What they're measured on.** Pull the actual comp plan. Is it retention, is it expansion, is it a hidden quota?

**What the customer experiences.** Where does the relationship actually land? Where does the "strategic check-in" go by minute forty?

The distance between the promise and the experience is your credibility debt. That gap is the real problem, and no title fixes it.

Then fix things in the right order. Build the deliverable first. Touch the comp plan second. Rename the role last, if you rename it at all. Most organizations do this exactly backwards. They rename first and hope the substance catches up.

## The harder, better path

The honest version of this transformation is slower and more expensive than a rebrand. It means building a real deliverable instead of borrowing a word. It means touching the compensation plan, which nobody wants to do. It means actually paying down the debt.

So here's my question for the leader with the reorg memo half-written. Your candidates went defensive at "customer success" for reasons that had nothing to do with the word itself. What makes you think your customers will stay comfortable with the new word once they discover what it's incentivised to do?

Rename the role if you want to fill the hiring funnel. Just don't confuse a full funnel with a paid-down debt. The customer keeps the ledger. And the customer collects at renewal.
