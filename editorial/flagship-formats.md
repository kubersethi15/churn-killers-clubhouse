# BC-04: flagship format portfolio

**By Claude, 25 August 2026.** These are reusable structures, not written
issues. Each defines the skeleton, the reader decision it serves, when to use
it, and how it scores against the BC-02 issue scorecard. Filling them with copy
is editorial work in Kuber's voice; that is not done here.

Why formats matter, from BC-02: the archive is excellent but shapeless. Every
issue is bespoke, so nothing is recognisable or nameable. Lenny's franchises get
shared because readers know what they are. Three named formats give Churn Is
Dead recurring shapes without diluting the one-decision-per-issue discipline.

Each format is designed to score 9+ on the 12-point scorecard by construction,
and each targets the two BC-02 gaps directly: a required diagram slot (visual
gap) and a required evidence line (original-evidence gap).

---

## Format 1: The Decision Teardown

**Serves:** an operator facing one specific, costly, recurring CS decision who
needs to make it better, not read theory.

**Use when:** the week's topic is a single decision with a clear owner and a real
cost of getting it wrong (renewal call, escalation threshold, expansion timing,
whether to fire a QBR).

**Skeleton:**

1. **The decision, named.** One sentence: who has to decide what, and by when.
2. **The default, and why it fails.** The move most teams make and the specific
   failure mode it walks into.
3. **The evidence threshold.** What has to be true before this decision is
   credible. This is the required evidence line.
4. **The trade-off nobody says out loud.** What the better decision costs, so
   the reader is not sold a free lunch.
5. **The owner.** Who is actually accountable, and what CS hands to them versus
   owns itself.
6. **One diagram.** The decision as a flow: inputs, the threshold gate, the two
   branches, the failure edge. Required, and it doubles as the LinkedIn asset.
7. **The tool.** A one-page decision record or checklist the reader runs this
   week. Links an existing playbook or ships a new one.

**Scorecard fit:** hits all six checks by design. The diagram satisfies check 5,
the evidence threshold satisfies check 4, the single named decision satisfies
checks 1 and 6.

## Format 2: The Operating System

**Serves:** a CS leader who wants to install a repeatable cadence or mechanism,
not just understand a concept.

**Use when:** the topic is a system a team can adopt (the 30-minute QBR, a value-
proof cadence, an intervention-trigger ledger).

**Skeleton:**

1. **The mechanism in one line.** What the system is, stripped to its core.
2. **The problem it replaces.** The current practice and its specific cost.
3. **The build.** The parts of the system and how they fit. Diagram required
   here: the system as a labelled loop or cadence, not a list.
4. **Facilitation steps.** Exactly how to run it the first time, including the
   pre-wire and the hardest moment.
5. **The adoption check.** How the reader knows it took, and the signal that it
   is reverting to the old practice.
6. **The tool.** The template, agenda, or worksheet that is the system, not a
   description of it.

**Scorecard fit:** check 3 (runnable tool) and check 5 (diagram) are structural.
The adoption check is the honest failure-mode content that separates this from a
vendor how-to.

## Format 3: The Evidence Brief

**Serves:** an operator who needs to know what is actually established versus
hype on a contested CS topic, and what to test next.

**Use when:** a topic is loud and under-evidenced (AI in CS, health scores,
NRR ownership) and the value is separating signal from noise.

**Skeleton:**

1. **The question.** The contested claim, stated as a question an operator has
   to answer for their own team.
2. **What credible sources agree on.** The settled part, each point sourced to
   the evidence ledger. This is where the format earns its name.
3. **What remains genuinely uncertain.** Named honestly, not hand-waved. The
   register that builds trust.
4. **What the hype gets wrong.** The specific overclaim and why it fails in a
   real account.
5. **One chart or diagram.** The evidence visualised: a comparison, a spectrum,
   a decision boundary. Required.
6. **What to test next.** One experiment the reader runs to get their own
   answer, since the brief will not have a universal one.

**Scorecard fit:** this is the format that most directly attacks the original-
evidence gap, because "what to test next" plus the quarterly pulse benchmark
(BC-02 change 1) turns readers into a data source over time.

## Cadence and rollout

Do not run all three at once. BC-02's test proposes one named franchise monthly.
Recommended order:

1. **Decision Teardown first.** It is closest to the existing best issues and
   the QBR post that drew 342 comments was effectively an un-named Teardown.
2. **Operating System second.** The 30-minute QBR and Value Proof Cadence are
   ready-made subjects.
3. **Evidence Brief once the pulse benchmark has run**, so it has original data
   to brief on rather than only synthesising others.

**Measurement (per BC-02 change 2):** run two editions of a format, compare
qualified actions and shares against the trailing four ordinary issues. Keep the
format if it lifts either; retire it if two editions do not.

## Boundary

These are skeletons. The voice, the argument, the sourcing, and the actual issue
belong to Kuber or the editorial lane. This document commits nothing to the
calendar and changes no published issue.
